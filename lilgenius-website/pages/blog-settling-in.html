Settling in · 6 min read

The first two weeks of playgroup, honestly

Nobody warns you how physical it feels to hand over a crying two-year-old and walk away. Here is what actually happens after the gate closes, based on watching several hundred children do it.

Week one: the shock of a new room

Some children walk in and never look back. Most do not. The common pattern is two or three cheerful days — the novelty phase — followed by a difficult fourth day when your child works out that this is going to happen every morning.

That fourth-day dip catches parents off guard and is the single most common moment we hear “maybe he isn’t ready”. Almost always, he is. It is a sign of understanding, not distress.

The long goodbye makes it worse

The hardest advice to follow: say goodbye once, clearly, and go. Coming back for one more hug restarts the clock, because your child learns that crying brings you back. A calm, confident, thirty-second goodbye is kinder than a tearful five-minute one, even though it feels colder.

Tell your child the truth about who is collecting them and when: “Aai will come after snack time.” Then be there when you said. Predictability does more for a two-year-old than reassurance.

What we do on our side

New children start with a shortened settling-in week — an hour or so at first, building up. A teacher is assigned as the child’s anchor for those days, so there is one familiar face to attach to rather than a rotating cast. We keep a comfort object if it helps, and we do not force participation in circle time.

You get a message once your child has settled, usually within fifteen minutes of drop-off. If they have not settled, you get that message too. We do not tell parents everything is fine when it is not.

Week two: the sneaky regression

Around day eight to twelve, several things often show up at home at once: broken sleep, a return to bedwetting, clinginess in the evening, or a sudden refusal to eat something they liked last month. This is normal. Your child is spending an enormous amount of energy holding it together in a new place, and home is where it gets released.

It usually settles within a week or two. What helps: earlier bedtimes than you think are necessary, a very boring predictable evening routine, and no big changes stacked on top — the month a child starts playgroup is not the month to also drop the nappy, move rooms, or travel.

How long should it take?

Most children are walking in comfortably within one to three weeks. Younger two-year-olds, and children who have never been apart from a parent, often take a little longer. That is a difference in starting point, not in ability.

When crying is a real signal

Settling difficulty stops being routine and starts being worth investigating if, after about a month, your child is still distressed during the session rather than only at the gate; if they refuse food and water all morning; if they can name a specific fear about the place; or if they were settled and then suddenly regress with no change at home.

None of these mean something is wrong. They mean the school should be looking closely and telling you what it sees. If you raise a concern like this and get a shrug, that is information about the school.

One thing that helps more than anything

Talk about school in the evening as an ordinary part of life rather than an achievement or an ordeal. Not “were you a brave girl today?” — just “what did you play with?”. Children take their emotional reading of a place largely from their parents. If you are relaxed about it, the odds go up considerably that they will be too.

Welcome

The first step to a better future starts here

We aim to shape the minds of our little geniuses by equipping them with critical thinking skills, a global perspective, and respect for the core values of honesty, loyalty and compassion.

Developing these skills is the cornerstone of the education we offer. In practice that means a child at Li’l Genius spends the morning building, sorting, questioning, singing and getting messy — and goes home able to do a little more on their own than they could yesterday.

We are a single-centre neighbourhood school, not a franchise. The person who answers your call is the person who will know your child’s name.

About Li’l Genius

A small school that knows every child by name

Li’l Genius Pre-School opened in Balewadi in 2019 with one classroom, a handful of children and a simple idea: young children learn best when they are known well.

We have grown since then, but deliberately slowly. We are still a single centre, still owner-run, and still small enough that the person who answers the phone knows which child is having a hard week and why.

Families come to us from Balewadi, Baner, Sus Road, Pashan and Wakad — mostly working parents who need a place that is genuinely good for their child and genuinely workable around a job.

Our vision

That every child who leaves Li’l Genius steps into Grade 1 curious rather than anxious — confident that questions are welcome, that mistakes are useful, and that they are capable of doing things for themselves.

Our mission

  • Keep groups small enough that no child is overlooked.
  • Teach through play, materials and conversation before pencil and paper.
  • Build honesty, kindness and responsibility into the daily routine, not into a poster.
  • Work as partners with parents, with nothing hidden.

Teaching philosophy

How we think about early learning

A two-year-old does not learn the concept of “three” by tracing it. They learn it by handing out three spoons, finding three stones, clapping three times, and being asked “how many now?” twenty times in a week. Our classrooms are set up for that kind of learning.

Play is the method, not the reward

Sand, water, dough, blocks, dress-up and role play are not what children do after the work is finished. For this age group they are the work. Formal writing is introduced only when a child’s hand and attention are ready, usually in Junior KG.

Independence over compliance

Children put on their own shoes, carry their own bag, pour their own water and clear their own plate — slowly and imperfectly at first. This is the practical meaning of our motto, “Do it your own the best”: do it yourself, and do it as well as you can.

Values are taught in the moment

Honesty, loyalty and compassion are learned when a four-year-old owns up to a broken toy, or comforts a friend who has been left out. We use those moments rather than lecture about them.

English with room for home language

The classroom runs in English, but Marathi and Hindi are never treated as a mistake. A child who is settling in is spoken to in whatever language helps them feel safe first.

Playgroup

2 to 3 years

The gentlest year. The goal is not academics — it is that your child walks in happily, separates from you without distress, and starts to trust an adult who is not family.

Nursery

3 to 4 years

Timing: 9:30 am to 12:30 pm, Monday to Friday.

Language takes off this year. Children move from single words to full sentences, and from parallel play to actual friendships.

Junior KG

4 to 5 years

Timing: 9:00 am to 12:30 pm, Monday to Friday.

Senior KG

5 to 6 years

Timing: 9:00 am to 1:00 pm, Monday to Friday.

The bridge to Grade 1. By the end of the year most children read short sentences, write independently and sit through a structured task without needing an adult beside them.

Daycare

Care that carries on until 7 pm

Daycare runs from the end of the school session until 7:00 pm, six days a week, and can be taken full-time or on selected days. It is a structured afternoon, not a waiting room.

  • Lunch supervision and a hygiene routine
  • A proper nap period with mats and a darkened room
  • Quiet activity, free play and outdoor time
  • Snack, and a settling-down period before pick-up

A day with us

What a morning actually looks like

Times shift a little by age group, but the shape of the day stays the same — predictable enough to feel safe, varied enough to stay interesting.

  1. Arrival and free play

    Children settle in at their own pace, greet their teacher and choose an activity while everyone arrives.

  2. Circle time

    The calendar, weather, news from home, a rhyme and the theme of the day. This is where most of the talking happens.

  3. Concept session

    The focused block: language or numbers, taught with materials the children handle themselves.

  4. Snack and washroom routine

    Hands washed, tiffin opened by the child wherever possible, plates cleared afterwards.

  5. Activity and outdoor play

    Art, music, movement, story or gross-motor play outdoors before the day winds down.

Curriculum

A written plan, taught with real materials

Our curriculum is activity-based and theme-led. Every fortnight has a theme — My Body, Water, Transport, Plants, Festivals, Helpers Around Us — and each of the six learning domains below is taught through it.

Language and literacy

Listening, speaking, vocabulary, phonics, pre-writing strokes and, later, reading and independent writing. Talk comes long before text.

Early mathematics

Counting with objects, one-to-one matching, sorting, patterns, shapes, measurement, and number operations built on things children can hold.

Understanding the world

Simple science and social awareness: living and non-living, seasons, plants, animals, our neighbourhood, festivals and the people who help us.

Physical development

Gross motor play outdoors, and fine motor work — threading, tearing, cutting, tweezers, clay — that quietly builds the hand strength writing needs.

Creative expression

Art with a range of media, music and rhythm, movement, and dramatic play. Process over product: we display what children made, not what a teacher finished.

Personal and social skills

Self-help, emotional vocabulary, waiting, sharing, resolving a disagreement with words, and the honesty and compassion our values rest on.

Weekly rhythm

Special activities through the week

  • Library day — each child chooses a book to take home.
  • Fireless cooking — simple no-heat recipes children assemble themselves.
  • Show and tell — two minutes of speaking to the group.
  • Music and movement — rhythm, dance and instrument play.
  • Messy play — the day to send old clothes.
  • Sports and outdoor games — structured group games.

Through the year

Events that punctuate the calendar

Festival celebrations through the year — Ganeshotsav, Navratri, Diwali, Christmas, Holi — alongside Independence Day, Grandparents’ Day, Sports Day, a field trip, an annual day performance and a graduation ceremony for Senior KG.

We also run short paid holiday workshops that are open to children from outside the school, such as craft and festival workshops for ages 3 to 9.

How we track progress

There are no exams and no ranks at this age — both are actively unhelpful. Instead teachers keep an observation record for each child against the six domains above, updated through the term.

Parents receive a written progress summary twice a year and a face-to-face meeting to go with it. If something is not going well, you will hear about it from us before you hear about it from your child.

A useful question to ask any preschool: “What does my child find difficult?” If the answer is “nothing at all”, nobody is watching closely enough.

Facilities

Everything at child height

A preschool building should let a four-year-old do as much as possible without asking an adult for help. That principle decided most of what is here.

Bright, smart classrooms

Naturally lit rooms with washable surfaces, rounded corners, low open shelving and child-sized furniture. Every room has a display board at the children’s eye level, not the teacher’s.

An audio-visual setup supports rhymes, stories and concept videos — used in short, planned doses rather than as a way to keep a room quiet.

Indoor and outdoor play

A well-equipped indoor play zone with soft flooring keeps the day moving through the Pune monsoon, with a ball pool, slides and a soft-block area.

Outdoors there is space for climbing, riding, running and sand play, supervised at all times, with shade for the summer months.

In-house library

Picture books, board books and early readers on shelves children reach themselves. Each child borrows a book weekly to read at home with a parent — the single highest-return ten minutes in a preschooler’s day.

Activity and art zone

A dedicated messy area for paint, clay, water and sand, so art does not have to be scaled down to protect the furniture.

Safety and security

What we do about safety, specifically

Vague reassurance is easy. Here is what actually happens.

CCTV coverage

Cameras across classrooms, corridors and play areas, with footage retained and reviewable by the management on request.

Single supervised entry

One gate, staffed through school hours, and no child released to anyone who is not on the authorised pick-up list.

Verified staff

Identity and reference checks before joining. All adults on campus, including support staff, are known and accounted for.

Paediatric first aid

A stocked first-aid kit, staff trained to use it, and an immediate call to parents for anything beyond a scraped knee — every time.

Daily hygiene

Sanitised toys and surfaces, supervised hand-washing before and after eating, and separate child-height washrooms.

Illness policy

Children with fever, conjunctivitis or a contagious infection stay home. It feels strict on the day and saves the whole class a fortnight later.

Frequently asked

Questions parents ask us

If yours is not here, call 090753 57058 — we would rather answer it properly than have you guess.

Timings and daily routine

What are the school and daycare timings?

We are open Monday to Saturday, 8:30 am to 7:00 pm, and closed on Sunday. Preschool sessions run in the morning — between 9:00 am and 1:00 pm depending on the programme — and daycare continues afterwards until 7:00 pm.

Do you provide meals?

Children bring a home-packed tiffin and a water bottle. Eating is supervised, and we follow a strict no-sharing rule so that allergies stay manageable. Please tell us in writing about any food allergy.

Is there transport?

We do not run our own school van. Most of our families live within a short drive and drop off themselves. Ask us at the time of admission about pooling options other parents have arranged.

What should my child bring every day?

A tiffin, a labelled water bottle, a spare set of clothes and, for the younger groups, a change of underwear and nappies if still needed.

Settling in

How long does it take a child to settle?

Usually one to three weeks. We start with a settling-in week of reduced hours, and parents can stay close by at first. Crying at drop-off in the first fortnight is normal and almost always stops within minutes of you leaving — we will tell you honestly if it does not.

Will you tell me how my child is doing during the day?

Yes. You get a daily update, and we call you the same day if anything notable happens — an injury, a difficult morning, or a first that we think you would want to know about.

My child is very shy. How do you handle that?

Slowly, and without forcing participation. A shy three-year-old who is watching from the edge is still learning. What we do not do is leave them there for weeks unnoticed — a 1:10 ratio exists precisely so that quiet children get attention too.

Safety and health

Is the campus CCTV monitored?

Yes. Classrooms, corridors and play areas are covered, and footage is retained and reviewable by the management. If you have a concern, raise it and we will look at it with you.

Who is allowed to collect my child?

Only people on the authorised pick-up list you give us at admission, whose photographs we keep on file. For a one-off change, call us in advance — a message from an unknown number is not enough.

What happens if my child falls ill at school?

We call you immediately, give basic first aid, and keep the child comfortable and separate from the group until you arrive. We do not administer any medication without your written instruction.

When should I keep my child at home?

Fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, conjunctivitis or any contagious rash. It is frustrating on the day, but it is the only thing that stops half a classroom being ill the following week.

Learning

Do you give homework?

Not in the conventional sense. Junior and Senior KG get short practice work a few times a week. Below that, the only thing we ask is that you read the weekly library book together.

Will my child be ready for Grade 1?

That is the explicit goal of Senior KG. By the end of the year most children read simple sentences, write independently, handle numbers to 100, and can sit through a structured task without an adult beside them.

Which language is used in class?

English is the classroom language. Marathi and Hindi are used freely to comfort or explain, especially while a child is settling in.

Choosing a school · 7 min read

Ten questions worth asking on a preschool tour

Every preschool tour in Pune shows you roughly the same things: bright walls, a play area, a wall of certificates. None of that tells you what your child’s Tuesday morning will feel like. These questions do.

1. How many children are in the room, and how many adults?

Ask for the number in the specific room your child would join, not the school average. A ratio of 1:10 means something quite different from 1:10 “including the helper and the principal”. If the answer is vague, ask to stand in the doorway and count.

2. How long has the class teacher been here?

Staff turnover is the most underrated signal in early education and the one nobody advertises. A school where teachers stay for years is almost always a school that treats its teachers well — and your two-year-old will not have to attach to a new adult every term.

3. What does the day actually look like, hour by hour?

A good answer is specific: arrival, circle time, a concept session, snack, activity, outdoor play. A weak answer is a list of adjectives. If forty minutes of the morning cannot be accounted for, it is usually screen time or waiting.

4. When do children start formal writing?

This is the question that most reliably splits preschools in two. A three-year-old pushed into handwriting produces neat books and a tired, discouraged hand. Pre-writing work — threading, tearing, clay, tweezers, big strokes — should come first, with letter formation arriving around Junior KG.

Be sceptical of a school that proudly shows you identical, beautifully finished artwork from a class of twenty three-year-olds. Somebody else finished it.

5. What happens when a child cries at drop-off in week three?

Everyone has an answer for week one. Week three is the real question, because it is when a school either investigates or starts saying “he is fine two minutes after you leave” on autopilot.

6. How and when will I hear from you?

Ask specifically: will I be called the same day if my child is hurt, however minor? Will I be told if she had a difficult morning, or only if there is good news? A school that only reports positives is managing you, not informing you.

7. Who can collect my child, and how do you verify it?

There should be an authorised pick-up list with photographs and a firm rule about one-off changes. If the answer is “we know all the parents”, that is a policy that works right up until the day it does not.

8. Can I see the washrooms?

Ask to see them, and to see where children eat. These two rooms are the honest ones. Reception areas are decorated for visitors; a washroom at 11 am is not.

9. What is your illness policy, and is it enforced?

A strict policy is inconvenient for you exactly once and saves your child several infections a year. A school that lets feverish children stay because parents have to work is not doing anyone a favour.

10. Can I speak to a parent from that class?

Any confident school will connect you. Five minutes with a current parent is worth more than an hour of anyone’s marketing, including ours.

Two more things, specific to Pune

First, visit in the monsoon if you can, or at least ask what happens to outdoor play in July. Schools with no usable indoor play space have a very long, very restless rainy season.

Second, work out the actual commute at 9 am on a weekday, not at 3 pm on a Sunday. A preschool ten minutes further away is fine; a school that turns into a forty-minute crawl through Baner traffic will wear down the whole family by October.

Settling in · 6 min read

The first two weeks of playgroup, honestly

Nobody warns you how physical it feels to hand over a crying two-year-old and walk away. Here is what actually happens after the gate closes, based on watching several hundred children do it.

Week one: the shock of a new room

Some children walk in and never look back. Most do not. The common pattern is two or three cheerful days — the novelty phase — followed by a difficult fourth day when your child works out that this is going to happen every morning.

That fourth-day dip catches parents off guard and is the single most common moment we hear “maybe he isn’t ready”. Almost always, he is. It is a sign of understanding, not distress.

The long goodbye makes it worse

The hardest advice to follow: say goodbye once, clearly, and go. Coming back for one more hug restarts the clock, because your child learns that crying brings you back. A calm, confident, thirty-second goodbye is kinder than a tearful five-minute one, even though it feels colder.

Tell your child the truth about who is collecting them and when: “Aai will come after snack time.” Then be there when you said. Predictability does more for a two-year-old than reassurance.

What we do on our side

New children start with a shortened settling-in week — an hour or so at first, building up. A teacher is assigned as the child’s anchor for those days, so there is one familiar face to attach to rather than a rotating cast. We keep a comfort object if it helps, and we do not force participation in circle time.

You get a message once your child has settled, usually within fifteen minutes of drop-off. If they have not settled, you get that message too. We do not tell parents everything is fine when it is not.

Week two: the sneaky regression

Around day eight to twelve, several things often show up at home at once: broken sleep, a return to bedwetting, clinginess in the evening, or a sudden refusal to eat something they liked last month. This is normal. Your child is spending an enormous amount of energy holding it together in a new place, and home is where it gets released.

It usually settles within a week or two. What helps: earlier bedtimes than you think are necessary, a very boring predictable evening routine, and no big changes stacked on top — the month a child starts playgroup is not the month to also drop the nappy, move rooms, or travel.

How long should it take?

Most children are walking in comfortably within one to three weeks. Younger two-year-olds, and children who have never been apart from a parent, often take a little longer. That is a difference in starting point, not in ability.

When crying is a real signal

Settling difficulty stops being routine and starts being worth investigating if, after about a month, your child is still distressed during the session rather than only at the gate; if they refuse food and water all morning; if they can name a specific fear about the place; or if they were settled and then suddenly regress with no change at home.

None of these mean something is wrong. They mean the school should be looking closely and telling you what it sees. If you raise a concern like this and get a shrug, that is information about the school.

One thing that helps more than anything

Talk about school in the evening as an ordinary part of life rather than an achievement or an ordeal. Not “were you a brave girl today?” — just “what did you play with?”. Children take their emotional reading of a place largely from their parents. If you are relaxed about it, the odds go up considerably that they will be too.

School readiness · 6 min read

Is my child ready for Grade 1?

Parents almost always ask this as an academic question. Grade 1 teachers, when you ask them, answer it as a self-management question. Both matter, and the second one is the half that gets neglected.

The academic half

By the end of Senior KG, most children are able to:

  • Recognise all letters and their sounds, and blend simple words
  • Read short, familiar sentences with some support
  • Write their own name and simple words without tracing
  • Count to 100, recognise numerals, and add and subtract small quantities
  • Identify shapes, colours, opposites and simple patterns
  • Speak in full sentences and describe something that happened yesterday

A child who is not yet fluent at reading is not behind. Reading fluency arrives across a wide age band, and pushing it earlier buys very little by Grade 3.

The half that decides how Grade 1 actually goes

Ask any Grade 1 teacher which children have a hard first term, and the answer is rarely about phonics. It is about these:

  • Managing themselves. Opening a tiffin, using the washroom independently, wiping, washing hands, drinking enough water, keeping track of a bag and a bottle.
  • Sustained attention. Staying with one task for fifteen to twenty minutes without an adult sitting alongside.
  • Following two-step instructions. “Put your book away and line up at the door” — heard once, done without prompting.
  • Asking for help. Being willing to tell an adult “I don’t understand” in a room of forty children.
  • Recovering from frustration. Getting something wrong, and continuing rather than shutting down.
  • Waiting. Tolerating not being first, not being chosen, and not being noticed for a whole hour.

What you can do in the last six months

Very little of it looks like academics. Stop doing the things your child can do slowly by themselves — shoes, buttons, packing the bag — even though it costs you ten minutes each morning. Read together daily and let them tell you what happened in the story. Give two-step instructions at home and wait rather than repeating.

If you are going to buy one thing for Grade 1 readiness, make it a tiffin your child can open alone. It sounds trivial. Ask any Grade 1 teacher how much of week one is spent opening lunch boxes.

About school interviews

Many Pune schools hold a short interaction for Grade 1 entry. In practice it is a conversation: name, family, colours, a picture to talk about, a rhyme. Children who have been rehearsed to answer in fixed sentences often do worse than children who chat comfortably, because the moment they get an unexpected question, the script fails. Prepare by talking to your child more, not by drilling answers.

If your child is not there yet

Being on the younger side of the cohort is genuinely disadvantageous in the first two years and largely evens out afterwards. If your child turns six well after school starts, and is struggling with attention and self-management rather than with letters, an extra year is a legitimate option and not a failure. Talk it through with the teacher who sees your child every day.
